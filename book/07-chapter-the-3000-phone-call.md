# Chapter 7: The $3,000 Phone Call

## The Script That Turns Strangers Into Paying Clients

Let me be real with you: the phone call (or Zoom call, or even a good email exchange) is where the money is made. Everything else — leads, spreadsheets, developers — is infrastructure. This conversation is the engine.

But here's the good news: you don't need to be a natural salesperson. You don't need charisma, charm, or a silver tongue. You need a simple script and the willingness to follow it.

I'm going to give you the exact script I use. It's not pushy. It's not slimy. It's a conversation between two humans where you ask questions, listen, and then offer to solve their problem.

### The 5-Phase Script

#### Phase 1: The Warm-Up (2 minutes)

The goal here is to be a human being. Not a salesperson. Not a telemarketer. A friendly person who's genuinely curious about their business.

> "Hey [Name], this is [Your Name]. Thanks for taking a minute to chat! I know you're busy, so I'll keep this quick. I came across [Business Name] and I was really impressed — [genuine compliment about their business]. How long have you been in business?"

Let them talk. People love talking about their business. Ask one or two follow-up questions.

> "That's awesome. [Response to what they said]. Sounds like you've built something really great."

#### Phase 2: The Discovery (3-5 minutes)

Now you're going to uncover their pain. You already know they need a website — but THEY need to say it.

> "So tell me, when new customers are looking for a [type of business] in [city], how do they usually find you?"

They'll say things like word of mouth, Google, social media, etc.

> "Nice. And do you have a website right now?"

Three possible answers:

**If NO:** "Got it. Have you ever thought about getting one? What's been holding you back?"

**If YES (but it's bad):** "I took a look at it — it's got good bones! When was the last time it was updated? How does it look on a phone?"

**If YES (and it's decent):** This is a harder sell. Focus on what's missing — SEO, speed, modern design, mobile optimization, or lead capture.

Then ask the money question:

> "If I asked you, on a scale of 1-10, how happy are you with your online presence right now... what would you say?"

If they say anything below 8, you have an opening. Most will say 3-6.

> "What would a 10 look like for you?"

Let them describe their dream website. Take notes. They're literally telling you what to sell them.

#### Phase 3: The Bridge (2 minutes)

Now you connect their pain to your solution.

> "That totally makes sense. You know, what you're describing is exactly what I help businesses like yours with. I work with a team of professional web designers, and we specialize in building websites for [their industry]. The goal is always the same — make it look incredible, make it easy for customers to find you, and make it bring in business."

Notice what you DIDN'T say:
- You didn't mention code, HTML, WordPress, or any technical jargon
- You didn't say "I build websites" — you said "I work with a team"
- You focused on RESULTS (look incredible, customers find you, brings in business)

#### Phase 4: The Show (3-5 minutes)

> "Let me show you a couple examples of what we've done for other [industry] businesses."

If you're on a phone call: "Can I text you a couple screenshots?"
If you're on Zoom: share your screen.
If you're in email: attach your portfolio PDF.

Show them 3-5 of your best examples that are similar to their industry. As you show each one, say:

> "This is a [type of business] we did recently. They wanted [something similar to what the prospect described]. Notice how clean it is on mobile — over 70% of people will find your business on their phone."

Let them react. When they say "that looks great" or "I love that," you're almost there.

#### Phase 5: The Close (2-3 minutes)

> "So based on what you've described, here's what I'd recommend. A professional 5-page website with [list 3-4 things they mentioned wanting]. We'd handle everything — design, development, content, mobile optimization, and getting it live. You don't have to worry about any of the technical stuff."

Pause. Let it sink in.

> "For a business like yours, a website like this typically runs around $2,500 to $3,500. And that includes everything — design, development, revisions, and launch."

Pause again. Let them respond.

> "We do a 50/50 setup — half upfront to get started, and the rest when you're 100% happy with the final site. That way there's zero risk for you. If you're not thrilled, you don't pay the second half. Sound fair?"

Most people will say one of three things:
1. **"That sounds great, let's do it."** — Collect the deposit. You just made $2,500+
2. **"Let me think about it."** — See objection handling below
3. **"That's more than I expected."** — See pricing objections below

## Handling Objections

Objections aren't rejections. They're speed bumps. Here's how to handle the most common ones:

### "I need to think about it."

> "Totally understand. Can I ask — what specifically do you want to think about? Is it the timing, the budget, or something about the project itself?"

This question uncovers the REAL objection. They usually say budget or timing. Then you can address it directly.

If it's budget: "I completely get it. We do have a more streamlined option starting at $1,500 that gives you a professional 3-page site. Would that be more in your range?"

If it's timing: "No rush at all. When would be a good time to circle back? I'll put a reminder on my calendar." (Put it in your Excel spreadsheet!)

### "I already have a website."

> "That's great! How's it working for you? Is it bringing in new customers the way you'd like?"

If they admit it's not great:
> "A lot of my clients were in the same spot. Their old site was actually turning customers away. A modern redesign can make a huge difference. Want me to show you a before-and-after from a similar business?"

### "That's too expensive."

> "I hear you, and I appreciate you being upfront about that. Let me ask you this — how many new customers per month would a professional website need to bring in to pay for itself?"

For most businesses, the answer is 1-3 new customers. Then:

> "So if your new website brings in just 2 extra customers per month at $[their average sale], it pays for itself in the first month or two. After that, it's pure profit. That's why most of my clients see their website as an investment, not a cost."

### "My nephew/friend/cousin can do it."

> "That's awesome that you have someone! Have they built sites for businesses before? The reason I ask is that a business website is really different from a personal site — it needs to show up on Google, look great on phones, load fast, and actually convert visitors into customers. That's what we specialize in."

If they push back: "No worries at all. If things don't work out with them, keep my number. I'm always here."

### "Can you do it cheaper?"

> "I understand wanting to get the best value. Here's what I can do — I can create a streamlined version with 3 pages instead of 5, which I can do for $1,800. Or, if you go with the full package, I'll include 3 months of free maintenance (normally $149/month). Which sounds better?"

Always give two options instead of just lowering your price. This maintains your value while giving them a feeling of choice.

## Why You NEVER Talk About Code, Hosting, or Tech Specs

This is critical and I need you to internalize it completely.

When you're talking to a business owner, NEVER say:
- "We'll build it in WordPress" — They don't care
- "It'll be hosted on Bluehost" — They don't know what that means
- "We'll use a responsive CSS framework" — Their eyes just glazed over
- "The site will be built with HTML5 and JavaScript" — You just lost the sale

Instead, say:
- "It'll look stunning on phones and computers"
- "It'll show up when people search for your business on Google"
- "It'll have a button where customers can call you with one tap"
- "We'll make it easy for you to update your hours and menu"

**Sell the outcome, not the process.** Business owners want to know: Will it look good? Will it bring customers? Will it be easy? That's it.

Think about it — when you buy a car, do you care what kind of bolts hold the engine together? No. You care about how it drives, how it looks, and how much it costs. Your clients feel the same way about websites.

## Selling the Result, Not the Product

The biggest mindset shift in this entire chapter:

**You are not selling a website. You are selling more customers.**

A restaurant owner doesn't want a website. She wants more diners.
A dentist doesn't want a website. He wants more patients.
A contractor doesn't want a website. He wants more jobs.

The website is the vehicle. The result is the destination. Always sell the destination.

When you pitch, use phrases like:
- "This will help new customers find you online"
- "Imagine someone searching for [their service] in [their city] and YOUR business shows up first"
- "A professional online presence builds trust before they even walk in the door"
- "Most people check a business's website before they decide to call — let's make sure yours impresses them"

When you frame it this way, $2,500-$5,000 isn't expensive. It's an investment that will return 10x in the first year. And that's exactly how your clients will see it.

## The Post-Call Checklist

After every call or meeting, immediately:

1. Update your Excel spreadsheet with the new status
2. Add detailed notes about what they want
3. Set a follow-up date
4. If they said yes, send a recap email:

> Subject: Excited to build [Business Name]'s new website!
>
> Hi [Name],
>
> Great chatting today! Just to recap:
>
> - 5-page professional website for [Business Name]
> - [List key features they requested]
> - Investment: $[Amount] (50% deposit to start, 50% on delivery)
> - Estimated delivery: 7-10 business days
>
> I'll send over the invoice for the deposit shortly. Once that's received, we'll kick things off right away.
>
> Looking forward to it!
>
> [Your Name]

5. Send the invoice (PayPal, Stripe, or a simple invoice template)
6. Once deposit is received, move them to your Active Projects tab
